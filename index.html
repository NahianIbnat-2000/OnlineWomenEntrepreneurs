Reading this in 2026? This research was conducted in Fall 2021, during the active COVID-19 pandemic period. All findings, percentages, and business figures reflect that specific moment in time. The landscape for online businesses in Bangladesh has shifted significantly since then.

Overview

What is this paper about?

COVID-19 pushed Bangladesh's unemployment rate from 4.20% in 2019 to 5.30% in 2020. But inside that crisis, something interesting happened: women across Chittagong started businesses online. This paper asks two simple questions: what pushed them to start, and can they keep going after the pandemic ends?

Using surveys from 50 women entrepreneurs in Chittagong City, the paper looks at factors like business type, household support, and family help, then tests whether those same factors shaped their decision to continue after COVID.

A note on class context. 66% of respondents had domestic help (housemaids). In Bangladesh, hiring domestic workers is largely a middle-class and upper-middle-class practice, concentrated in urban areas. ILO estimates place the total domestic workforce in Bangladesh at around 2 million, almost entirely employed by urban households. This means the sample in this paper skews toward women who already had economic advantages: smartphones, internet access, time freed up by paid help, and family networks that could support a new business. The online entrepreneurship boom documented here was not equally available to all women in Chittagong. It was largely a middle-class phenomenon.

The Respondents

Who are these entrepreneurs?

The 50 respondents ranged in age from 16 to 55. Most were young, married or single, and almost all were running their businesses entirely on Facebook or other social platforms.

Key Findings

What the data showed

Four main factors came up as important: COVID itself, type of business, access to a housemaid, and family support. Here is what each one looked like in practice.

🧣

Clothing leads everything

50% of respondents ran cloth and jewellery businesses. These are low-cost to start, easy to photograph and sell on Facebook, and already familiar to buyers.

🏠

Housemaids free up time

66% had household help. With chores handled, women could spend 21 to 42 hours a week on their business. 50% spent zero hours on housework.

👨‍👩‍👧

Family fills many roles

64% got family support. The top form of help was marketing (28%), followed by taking care of household chores (24%) and delivering products (22%).

📈

Profit is modest but satisfying

The median monthly profit was BDT 10,001 to BDT 20,000. Still, 42% said they were very satisfied with what they were earning, and only one person rated dissatisfaction.

💬

Customers respond well

50% said customers actively liked their products. The correlation between customer response and profit satisfaction was 0.61, a moderate positive link.

🔮

Most plan to keep going

78% said yes to continuing after COVID. 84% believed it was actually possible. The main uncertainty was whether customers would stay engaged without lockdown driving them online.

Literature Review

What research from other countries found

Before this paper, most studies on women entrepreneurs during COVID focused on how businesses collapsed, not how new ones formed. This paper fills a gap specific to Bangladesh. Here is what the existing literature looked like.

Garfin (2020)

Technology as a coping tool during COVID-19

The pandemic created widespread social disruption, closing schools and shuttering businesses. Digital technology emerged as a key coping mechanism, reducing anxiety and keeping people connected through social media platforms.

Apăvăloaie (2014)

The internet changes how business works

Digital technology motivates people to start businesses on their own. What experts predicted in 2006, that the internet would become the primary low-cost network for transactions, proved exactly right during the pandemic.

Ahmad, Ahmad & Bakar (2018)

Social media drives small business growth

Small businesses use social media to showcase products, build fan pages, and attract buyers. Sustained customer satisfaction through quality products builds long-term business relationships, which is especially critical for online-only sellers.

Hossain & Rahman (2018)

Online business opens doors for women

Women prefer online business because they can manage household responsibilities and business at the same time. Social media opportunities motivate women to start their own ventures, and this pattern held strongly in Bangladesh.

Jaim (2021)

COVID pushed many Bangladeshi women out of business

For women running traditional businesses in Bangladesh, COVID was harmful. The triple burden of business, household work, and child care became too much. This paper focused on that group, but left the online segment unexplored.

Manolova et al. (2020)

Pivoting to online saved some businesses

Women entrepreneurs who shifted from physical to online business during COVID actually experienced a rise in supply and demand. The pandemic forced a model change that, for many, turned out to be a better fit.

Afshan, Shahid & Tunio (2021)

Online training created new entrepreneurs in developing countries

Women who did online business courses during COVID learned production, marketing, delivery, and transaction management. The pandemic created a wave of first-time entrepreneurs in developing countries who had no prior business experience.

Muhammad et al. (2021)

Business type matters a lot

In Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa community, COVID was a boost for cloth and cosmetics sellers but a setback for women in dairy. Not all businesses respond to a pandemic the same way. Sector matters.

No literature had been published on Bangladeshi women entrepreneurs operating specifically on online platforms before this paper. This study fills that gap directly.

Research gap identified in the paper's literature review

Methodology

How the study was done

This was a quantitative study using convenience sampling, meaning respondents were women entrepreneurs the researcher followed on Facebook. Questionnaires were sent via Google Forms, Messenger, and WhatsApp. Both close-ended and open-ended questions were used. Data was analyzed in STATA and Google Sheets.

Analysis Types
  • Univariate (frequency tables, bar charts)
  • Bivariate (contingency tables, Cramer's V, Pearson's r, Spearman's rho)
  • Multivariate (OLS regression)
Key Variables
  • Business type (Nominal)
  • Availability of housemaid (Dichotomous)
  • Family support (Nominal)
  • Continuity decision (Nominal, dependent)
Regression Model
  • Continuity = β₁ + β₂ BusiType + β₃ HM + β₄ FamSup + ε
  • R-squared: 14.5%
  • F-test: not significant (F-calc < F-critical)
  • No heteroscedasticity (White test p = 0.66)
Validity Notes
  • Internal validity: not possible (no RCT)
  • External validity: limited to Chittagong
  • Reliability: results are consistent with international literature

Discussion

What the findings mean

The data points to three clear stories: what pushed women into online business, what shapes their decision to stay, and whether staying is actually realistic.

01

COVID was the push, not just the context

60% of respondents started their business during COVID. That is not a coincidence. Lockdowns removed the option of traditional employment, schools closed, and social media usage jumped. Women who had always been interested in selling but never had the time or reason found themselves with both. The pandemic did not create the ambition; it removed the barriers that had been in the way.

Cloth and jewellery dominated at 50% because the barrier to entry is low. You need a phone camera, a Facebook page, and a product. No shop, no rent, no staff. That accessibility matters a lot when you are starting from a tight budget, as most of these women were, with median investment below BDT 20,000.

02

Support frees up time, but does not decide the future

66% had household help and 64% had family support. These two things gave women the hours they needed to actually run a business. But when the regression model tested whether those same factors predicted the decision to continue after COVID, they did not. The F-test was not significant and R-squared sat at just 14.5%.

This is the most interesting result in the paper. The factors that helped women start and sustain their business during COVID are not the same ones that drive their future plans. What actually shapes continuation is likely something harder to measure: confidence, customer loyalty, income dependence, and how much the business became part of their identity during the pandemic.

03

The optimism is real, but so is the uncertainty

84% said continuation is possible and 78% said they want to continue. Those numbers are high. But 10% were unsure, and their reason was telling: they started online and had no idea how to run a traditional business. Their entire customer base was built on pandemic-era online behavior. Whether those customers stick around once life goes back to normal is a genuine question.

The strong correlation between customer response and profit satisfaction (0.61) and between customers and profit satisfaction (0.67) confirms that these businesses live and die by their relationship with buyers. As long as buyers keep coming back, the women plan to keep going. That dependency is both their strength and their main risk.

Conclusion

So, did it work out for them?

For most of these women, yes. They started with low investment (often under BDT 20,000), got real customers through Facebook, and earned profits that most of them were satisfied with. The pandemic gave them a reason to start, and the support from family and housemaids gave them the time to do it.

The weak correlation between the study's factors (business type, housemaid, family support) and the decision to continue suggests there are other things driving that decision, things this paper did not measure. That is where future research should go, with larger samples, better sampling methods, and more rigorous models.

This paper is best read as a starting point, not a final answer. It was one of the first to look at online women entrepreneurs in Bangladesh during COVID-19, and that alone gives it value.

💡 A question worth asking in 2026

It has been five years since this survey was conducted. If you still have contact with any of these 50 women, a short follow-up study could be very telling. Some questions worth asking:

  • Is the business still running? If not, when did it close and why?
  • Did revenue hold up after COVID restrictions lifted, or did customers drift back to physical shops?
  • Did any of them shift platforms, from Facebook to TikTok, Instagram, or a formal e-commerce site?
  • Did the domestic help situation change? Did losing a housemaid affect the business?
  • Did any of them hire employees, expanding beyond a solo operation?

Even a 20-question Google Form sent to the original contacts would create a small but genuine panel dataset: the same people, two time points, five years apart. That kind of longitudinal data on micro-entrepreneurship in Bangladesh is almost nonexistent in the published literature.
